This Is Not a Case Study

A short note on what this page is — and what it isn't.

We have no client relationship with T2, Kuda, or Mama Cass. These are public teardowns — our analysis of brands we don't work with, drawn from public reporting and company statements. Everything under The situation and What they did is fact. Everything from What worked onward is our opinion — informed, clearly ours, and no less useful for being opinions.

The clearest way to demonstrate how we think is to show our thinking on brands you already know. If you're looking for the work we've actually delivered, that lives in our portfolio and in conversations with clients — never in invented case studies.

Scenario 01 Telecoms · Nigeria

The brand that changed its name twice in eight years.

T2's rebrand is the most watched identity move in Nigerian telecoms. It's also the clearest lesson in what a new name can — and can't — do.

The Situation

9mobile spent its first decade as Etisalat Nigeria — green branding, an iconic "9", one of the country's most recognisable telecom identities. In 2017, a $1.2 billion debt crisis forced Etisalat Group out and a consortium of Nigerian banks took control. The brand was renamed 9mobile. It held on, but not comfortably. Subscribers fell from a peak above 20 million to under 3 million by 2025, according to industry reporting. In 2023, Lighthouse Telecoms acquired the business. By August 2025, it had a new name: T2.

What They Did

T2 launched with a full identity change. Most visibly, the green that defined 9mobile was replaced with orange — framed by the company as a symbol of "ripe arrival" after years of turbulence. The rebrand sat inside a four-phase recovery roadmap: Stabilisation, Modernisation, Transformation, Growth. This wasn't just a new logo. It was positioning the brand as post-crisis.

What Worked

The rebrand did something genuinely difficult: it gave the business a visible reset point. WARC's reporting on the launch noted T2's first subscriber gain in 20 months and improved stakeholder sentiment — which suggests that a well-executed, PR-led identity shift can move perception even while the underlying product is still being rebuilt. That's not nothing. In a market where trust is the currency, buying yourself a moment of attention is a real asset.

What No Rebrand Can Fix

At the launch, the Minister of Communications, Dr. Bosun Tijani, said the quiet part out loud: "Let this rebrand be more than a change of colours or new logo. Let it be a renewed commitment to innovation, service excellence, and the millions of Nigerians whose lives and businesses depend on your network."

That is the whole problem in one sentence. A new name can reset perception. It cannot reset a network. Subscribers who left because of dropped calls, weak data, and service failures do not come back because the logo is orange. They come back because the product works — and they tell their friends.

The VisOut Read

If this were our brief, we'd start by arguing that identity is not the turnaround plan. The identity is the promise the turnaround plan is making. So the sequence matters: fix what's broken, then re-announce yourself. Or — if the reset has to happen before the product is ready — make the promise explicit and time-bound. "This is who we're becoming. Here's when it lands." That way a rebrand buys patience, not distrust.

What we'd never do: treat a rebrand as a rescue. If the product is still failing, a new identity just gets blamed faster.

Scenario 02 Fintech · Nigeria

The fintech that won on "free" — and then had to find something bigger.

Kuda's first identity was built on a promise competitors could copy. Its second one had to be about something they couldn't.

The Situation

Kuda launched in 2019 as "the bank of the free" — no transfer fees, no queues, no branches. Purple brand, standalone "K", a tone built for a generation that had given up on traditional banks. It worked. For a while, that was all you needed to say.

Then the market caught up. Free transfers became standard. Mobile-first became standard. No-branches became standard. The differentiator wasn't wrong — it just stopped being different.

What They Did

In 2026, Kuda unveiled its biggest identity overhaul since launch: a full wordmark, a new typeface, an expanded palette (purple stays, plus lavender, off-white, and a darker grey), and photography of everyday Nigerian life instead of the old banking cues — cards, phones, branches. New positioning: "More Life."

The insight, as Kuda's senior brand manager put it, was that the customer had moved on from the original pitch: "People are no longer just trying to break free in 2026. People are trying to move up, to rise financially, socially, professionally... They are trying to live more."

What Worked

The move from breaking free to rising is a serious strategic shift, not a cosmetic one. It reframes Kuda from "the app you use to avoid bank fees" to "the app you use for everything you're building." That's a much bigger claim — and a much harder one to copy, because it depends on the customer's whole relationship with money, not a single feature.

What No Rebrand Can Fix

A bigger promise creates a bigger obligation. "More Life" means Kuda now has to help people build wealth, not just spend freely. That's a much harder product problem than "no transfer fees" — and if the app doesn't actually deliver on the new positioning, the identity starts to look like an aspiration the company can't yet fund.

The VisOut Read

If this were our brief, we'd treat it as a "second act" problem, not a redesign problem. The old sentence served its time. The work is finding the next sentence — one that can hold for five years, not one that describes what every competitor already does. Then design the identity so it can stretch to meet that sentence without being rebuilt in eighteen months.

What we'd never do: redesign the visuals before the new sentence is agreed. A prettier identity carrying the old pitch is just an expensive way to stand still.

Scenario 03 QSR · Nigeria

The restaurant that taught Nigeria how to eat out — and had to grow up without forgetting it.

Mama Cass has been feeding Lagos since 1983. Its relaunch is the textbook heritage problem: how much can you change before the people who loved you stop recognising you?

The Situation

Mama Cass opened on Allen Avenue, Ikeja, in 1983 — serving jollof, pounded yam, ofada rice, beans, and traditional soups at a time when "quick service restaurant" wasn't really a Nigerian category. It helped create one. Four decades later, it's trying to stay relevant in a market that has changed around it: younger urban consumers, foreign chains selling burgers and pizza, and a new generation of indigenous brands building modern identities on Nigerian food.

What They Did

After a period of strategic restructuring, Mama Cass relaunched with a refreshed identity, a new positioning as "The Home for Food Lovers," upgraded outlets, a refined menu, and a customer experience designed to blend what the company calls "nostalgia with modern efficiency." The company framed the shift carefully: "We have not changed from what we used to be. We have only evolved into something better."

What Worked

That framing is the most strategic line in the whole relaunch. It tells long-time customers they haven't been abandoned, and it tells new customers they're not walking into a museum. It also gives the brand a defensible middle ground — modern enough for a 25-year-old in Lekki, rooted enough for the 45-year-old who has been eating there since university.

What No Rebrand Can Fix

Heritage brands carry a structural risk that newer brands don't: the existing customer base is an asset and a brake. Change too little and you fade; change too much and you alienate the people whose loyalty funded the rebrand. The physical experience has to do most of the work — the food, the space, the service — because identity can only amplify what's already there. If the relaunch promise is "evolved into something better," then the proof is on the plate, not in the logo.

The VisOut Read

If this were our brief, we'd frame the work as evolution with guardrails: keep the elements that carry memory, change the elements that carry friction. Start from a clear answer to "what do our oldest customers love about us, and what do our newest customers wish we'd fix?" Then design only the second half. The first half is not design work. It's heritage management.

What we'd never do: let a rebrand quietly erase the details that made people love the brand in the first place. If a customer has been coming for 30 years, the rebrand has to feel like recognition, not replacement.

The Process

How an Engagement Runs

Four phases. Each one hands off to the next, and nothing starts before the phase before it is signed off.

Phase 01

Interrogate

We argue with you about what your brand is for. If we agree instantly, we haven't gone deep enough. This is where the real brief gets written — by both of us, not by either of us alone.

Phase 02

Commit

We put the answer in one sentence and get it signed off in writing. The logo doesn't move until the sentence is agreed. If the sentence is wrong, the visuals can't rescue it.

Phase 03

Build the System

Visual identity and video system together — not one, then the other — so your brand looks the same in a static post and a 15-second reel. This is the phase most agencies split into two projects, and it's why most brands break in motion.

Phase 04

Teach

We don't hand over files. We hand over a manual, a training session, and the rules for using them. You leave with everything you need to run the brand without us — and we're still on call for the first few months of rollout.

FAQ

Before You Read Us Wrong

A few honest clarifications about this page.

Did you work on these brands?
No. We have no client relationship with T2, Kuda, or Mama Cass. These are public teardowns — we analyse brands we don't work with because it's the clearest way to show how we think.
Are these facts or opinions?
Both, and clearly separated. The situation and what-they-did sections draw from public reporting and company statements. Everything from "what worked" onward is our opinion — informed, but ours.
Why analyse brands you don't work with?
Because anyone can show you their wins. The harder test is whether they can show you how they think. Teardowns let a reader judge our judgment without needing our client list.
Can you do this kind of analysis for my brand?
Yes. It's usually where a project starts — a focused audit of how your brand is currently being read versus how you want it to be read, followed by what to fix first.
How do you choose which brands to analyse?
Brands at a strategic turning point — a trust crisis, a relevance crisis, or a heritage crisis. Never brands we have a commercial relationship with, and never without using publicly available sources.
Will you publish a teardown of my brand?
Only with written permission. We don't publish analysis of client brands without sign-off, and we don't do it as a marketing tactic. Client work stays private unless you say otherwise.
